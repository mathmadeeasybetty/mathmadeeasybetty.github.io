Why Can You Split a Square Root Over Multiplication, but Not Addition?

Is √(a + b) Equal to √a + √b? Why Square Roots Don't Split Over Addition

By the end of this article, you'll understand how to simplify square roots, when you can split a radical, and why multiplication and addition follow different rules.

November 9, 2026
Try it first

Are these two expressions equal? Work out both before you read on.

$\sqrt{9 + 16}$
$\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$

Show the answer

$\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, but $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$.

They are not equal. If you thought they were, this is one of the most common algebra mistakes, and the rest of this article shows why it happens.

A square root can be split over multiplication and division. It cannot generally be split over addition or subtraction. We start with the case that fails, because that is where the mistake happens, then look at the case where splitting does work.

In this article, $a$ and $b$ are positive numbers, so every square root is a real number.

01 · Addition: where the split fails

Take $36 + 64$. Add inside the root first:

$\begin{aligned}\sqrt{36 + 64} &= \sqrt{100} \\ &= 10\end{aligned}$

Now compare that with splitting the root before adding:

The Trap

Splitting over addition

Common mistake

$\sqrt{36 + 64}$
$= \sqrt{36} + \sqrt{64}$
$= 6 + 8$
$= 14$

Correct way

$\sqrt{(36 + 64)}$
$= \sqrt{100}$
$= 10$

Since $10 \neq 14$, splitting the radical breaks the math:

$\begin{aligned}\sqrt{a+b} &\neq \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b} \\[2pt] &\text{in general}\end{aligned}$

The two sides are equal only in special cases: when $a=0$ or $b=0$. These exceptions do not make splitting a sum a valid general rule.

02 · Subtraction fails the same way

The Trap

Splitting over subtraction

Common mistake

$\sqrt{100 - 36}$
$= \sqrt{100} - \sqrt{36}$
$= 10 - 6$
$= 4$

Correct way

$\sqrt{(100 - 36)}$
$= \sqrt{64}$
$= 8$

Since $4 \neq 8$, splitting the radical gives an incorrect result:

$\begin{aligned}\sqrt{a-b} &\neq \sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b} \\[2pt] &\text{in general}\end{aligned}$

The two sides are equal in special cases: when $b=0$ or when $a=b$.

Here we also need $a \geq b$, so the number under the root is not negative.

03 · Where does the extra value come from?

If splitting over addition were valid, squaring the result would give us back the original number inside the radical: $36 + 64 = 100$.

$\begin{array}{l} (\sqrt{36} + \sqrt{64})^2 \\[4pt] = (6 + 8)^2 \\[4pt] = 6^2 + 2(6)(8) + 8^2 \\[4pt] = 36 + 96 + 64 \\[4pt] = 196 \end{array}$

We get $196$, not $100$. The extra $96$ comes from the middle term, $2(6)(8)$, created when we square a sum. This is why we cannot split a square root over addition.

Subtraction has the same issue, but the middle term is negative. If we split $\sqrt{100 - 36}$, we get $10 - 6 = 4$. Squaring that result should give us $100 - 36 = 64$, but watch what happens:

$\begin{array}{l} (\sqrt{100} - \sqrt{36})^2 \\[4pt] = (10 - 6)^2 \\[4pt] = 10^2 - 2(10)(6) + 6^2 \\[4pt] = 100 - 120 + 36 \\[4pt] = 16 \end{array}$

We get $16$, not $64$. The middle term is now $-120$. So square roots cannot be split over subtraction either.

04 · Multiplication and division: where splitting works

You have seen that sums and differences fail. Now test a product.

Try it first

Are these two expressions equal? Work out both before you read on.

$\sqrt{4 \times 49}$
$\sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{49}$

Show the answer

$\sqrt{4 \times 49} = \sqrt{196} = 14$ and $\sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{49} = 2 \times 7 = 14$.

They match. A product is the case where splitting is safe, and the next section shows it with more examples.

Let's compare the two ways of working with multiplication.

First, multiply inside the radical:

$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{9 \times 25} &= \sqrt{225} \\ &= 15 \end{aligned}$

Now split the factors and simplify each square root:

$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{9} \times \sqrt{25} &= 3 \times 5 \\ &= 15 \end{aligned}$

Both methods give $15$. This time, splitting works:

$\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$

Why does it work? Square the split result:

$\begin{aligned} (\sqrt{9} \times \sqrt{25})^2 &= 9 \times 25 \\ &= 225 \end{aligned}$

We get back the original product, $225$. There is no extra middle term like the one created when squaring a sum or difference.

Division works the same way

Let's test division using the same approach.

First, divide inside the radical:

$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\frac{100}{4}} &= \sqrt{25} \\ &= 5 \end{aligned}$

Now split the numerator and denominator:

$\begin{aligned} \frac{\sqrt{100}}{\sqrt{4}} &= \frac{10}{2} \\ &= 5 \end{aligned}$

Both methods give $5$. Splitting works here too:

$\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}$

Squaring the split result confirms it:

$\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{\sqrt{100}}{\sqrt{4}}\right)^2 &= \frac{100}{4} \\ &= 25 \end{aligned}$

We get back the original quotient, $25$. Unlike addition and subtraction, splitting over multiplication and division gives us exactly the original value.

05 · So what do you do instead?

When addition or subtraction appears inside a radical, work it out first. Then check whether the resulting number has a perfect-square factor.

For example, take $\sqrt{9 + 25}$. Add inside first:

$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{9 + 25} &= \sqrt{34} \end{aligned}$

$34$ has no perfect-square factor greater than $1$, so the radical cannot be simplified further. Leave it as $\sqrt{34}$.

But sometimes the result does have a perfect-square factor. Look at this example:

$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{16 + 4} &= \sqrt{20} \\ &= \sqrt{4 \times 5} \\ &= \sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{5} \\ &= 2\sqrt{5} \end{aligned}$

Notice the order: add first, then rewrite $20$ as a product. Now you can split the multiplication and simplify.

Remember: You cannot split the original sum, but you can factor the result into a product.

A picture also helps. In a right triangle with legs $3$ and $4$, the squares of the legs are $9$ and $16$. Walking along both legs covers $3 + 4 = 7$, but the straight path across is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

$\begin{array}{c}\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 \\[2pt] \text{but} \\[2pt] \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 7\end{array}$

The Triangle Inequality Theorem states that the sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side. The shortcut across is shorter than the walk along both legs.

Your turn

Three quick checks. For each one, decide whether the root can be split before you open the answer.

A. Can this be split?

$\sqrt{64 \div 16}$

Show the answer

Yes, because it is division. Both ways give the same answer:

$\sqrt{64} \div \sqrt{16} = 8 \div 4 = 2$ and $\sqrt{64 \div 16} = \sqrt{4} = 2$

B. Can this be split?

$\sqrt{25 + 144}$

Show the answer

No, because it is addition. Work inside first:

$\sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$

Splitting gives $5 + 12 = 17$, which is the trap.

C. Can this be split?

$\sqrt{169 - 25}$

Show the answer

No, because it is subtraction. Work inside first:

$\sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$

Splitting gives $13 - 5 = 8$, which is the trap.

Quick checklist

  1. Addition: Cannot split. $\sqrt{36 + 64} \neq \sqrt{36} + \sqrt{64}$
  2. Subtraction: Cannot split. $\sqrt{100 - 36} \neq \sqrt{100} - \sqrt{36}$
  3. Multiplication: Can split. $\sqrt{4 \cdot 49} = \sqrt{4} \cdot \sqrt{49}$
  4. Division: Can split. $\sqrt{\dfrac{100}{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{100}}{\sqrt{4}}$
