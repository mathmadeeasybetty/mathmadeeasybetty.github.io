Algebra & Exponent Rules

Why −32 is Not 9 (And Why Your Calculator Isn't Broken)

Your calculator says −9. You expected 9. Here is the exponent rule behind the difference, and the one place where it flips the other way.

August 29, 2026 · 3 min read

Type $-3^2$ into Google or a TI-84 and you get $-9$. Type $(-3)^2$ and you get $9$. Same three symbols, two different answers.

Most students assume the calculator has a bug, or that the notation contradicts itself. After all, a negative times a negative is positive. So why doesn't $(-3) \times (-3) = 9$ apply here?

It does. It just isn't what you typed.

The Hidden Distinction: Unary Minus vs. Grouping

The minus sign in $-3^2$ is not part of the number. It is an operation in its own right, called a unary minus, and all it means is multiply by $-1$.

Texas Instruments describes it in exactly those terms: their calculators read $-3^2$ as $(-1) \times 3^2$.

Once you see that minus sign as a multiplication, the rule stops being arbitrary. Exponents outrank multiplication, so the squaring happens first and the $-1$ is applied to whatever comes out. The exponent only ever touches the number directly beneath it, unless parentheses tell it otherwise.

The Exponent Trap

Evaluating $-3^2$ vs. $(-3)^2$

$-3^2$ (without parentheses)

The exponent applies ONLY to $3$:
$-(3^2)$
$-(3 \times 3)$
$= \mathbf{-9}$

$(-3)^2$ (with parentheses)

The exponent applies to EVERYTHING inside:
$(-3) \times (-3)$
$= \mathbf{9}$

The One Place This Rule Flips

There is a real exception, and it catches students doing homework in a spreadsheet.

Type =-3^2 into Excel and it returns $9$, the opposite of every calculator. Google Sheets does the same thing.

This is not a bug either. Microsoft's own documentation on operator precedence states that negation is evaluated before exponentiation, which is the reverse of standard mathematical convention. If you need the mathematically correct result in a spreadsheet, write =-(3^2) or =-POWER(3,2).

Knowing this rule and remembering it mid-exam are two different things. The students who stop losing marks here are the ones who have worked through enough of these to spot the missing parentheses on sight.

Why This Matters on Algebra Exams

This small notation difference becomes a real trap the moment you substitute a negative value into an expression.

If an exam question asks you to evaluate $x^2$ when $x = -3$:

  • Correct: substitute the whole value inside parentheses, $(-3)^2 = \mathbf{9}$.
  • The common slip: writing $-3^2$ without them, which is a different expression and gives $\mathbf{-9}$.

Quick Checklist to Avoid Calculator Mistakes

  1. Wrap every substitution in parentheses. To evaluate $x^2$ at $x = -5$, write $(-5)^2$, never $-5^2$.
  2. Exponents beat the minus sign. The minus in $-x^2$ is a multiplication by $-1$, and exponents come before multiplication.
  3. Trust the order. On paper and on any calculator, $-x^2$ means $-(x^2)$. Spreadsheets are the one exception.
